Staccato AI: An Independent Producer's Review

By Justin Ray · Pioneer of Hybrid AI Music Production East Lansing, Michigan Filed under Field Notes

Staccato AI is a MIDI co-writer that lives inside a DAW instead of a browser tab, built by Dr. Jeffrey Lupker and Jason Kowalczyk out of Western University's Don Wright Faculty of Music. It doesn't generate a finished track. It generates editable chord progressions, drum patterns, and multitrack MIDI stems, then hands sound design, mixing, and arrangement back to the producer entirely. Justin Ray evaluated the tool within a Hybrid AI Music Production workflow across a series of studio sessions in East Lansing, judging it the way any working producer judges a new piece of gear: does it save time, does it make the track better, and is it worth paying for. After enough sessions to trust the pattern, the answer to all three was yes.

From a dissertation to a DAW plugin

Staccato AI began as a doctoral research problem rather than a startup pitch, and its architecture still traces back to a string quartet. While completing a PhD in composition at Western University's Don Wright Faculty of Music, Jeffrey Lupker needed a collaborator for a 25-minute piece and could not afford to hire one, so he built deep learning software that could suggest musical continuations instead. That research project became the company's first working version. Lupker later partnered with Jason Kowalczyk, a forensic accountant turned business co-founder, to bring the tool to market through Western's startup accelerator.

That academic lineage shows up in the product itself. Staccato is not trained to imitate finished pop records. The company states it trains on royalty-free, theory-grounded MIDI data, according to Staccato's own product documentation. That grounding in music theory rather than scraped audio is the whole reason the next section matters.

How it actually sits in a session

Staccato ships as a VST3, AU, and AAX plugin plus a standalone application for macOS and Windows, with confirmed integrations including Ableton Live, Logic Pro, Pro Tools, and FL Studio, per KVR Audio's and G2's product listings. The local footprint is small, around 4 GB of RAM and 50 MB of disk space, because generation happens on Staccato's servers rather than the local machine, according to Thomann's published system requirements. That tradeoff cuts both ways. It keeps the plugin lightweight, and it makes a stable internet connection mandatory for every single generation.

The interface pairs a chat window with a piano roll. Type a prompt, wait a few seconds, preview the result through your own virtual instruments, and drag the preferred take onto the timeline. Four operations cover most of the workflow.

[ CREATE ]

Builds original loops and chord progressions from a text prompt, outputting up to 16 tracks and 32 bars per pass.

[ ACCOMPANY ]

Analyzes an uploaded MIDI clip and writes complementary parts that match its key and tempo.

[ REWRITE ]

Restyles or humanizes an existing part without discarding its underlying harmony.

[ EXTEND ]

Continues a short loop into a longer arrangement, governed by Creativity and Complexity settings that control how far the model strays from the source.

Why comparing this to Suno misses the point

The two tools get lumped together because both have "AI" and "music" in the pitch, and that's about where the similarity ends. Suno and Udio are audio generators. You type a prompt and get back a finished, rendered song, complete with vocals, instrumentation, mix, and master, all decided by the model. Staccato is a symbolic generator. It never touches audio. It outputs MIDI data that means nothing until you route it through your own instruments, so every tone, every mix decision, and every arrangement choice is still yours.

That difference is also playing out in court. In June 2024, the RIAA filed copyright infringement suits against Suno and Udio on behalf of Universal Music Group, Sony Music Entertainment, and Warner Records, seeking damages of up to $150,000 per infringed work and describing the alleged conduct as "unlicensed copying of sound recordings on a massive scale", in the words of RIAA chief legal officer Ken Doroshow. Staccato, trained on royalty-free MIDI rather than scraped commercial recordings, sits entirely outside that dispute. It was never in the same category to begin with.

Pricing, and the case for buying instead of subscribing

Staccato runs on a subscription model, and the exact numbers shift depending on where you look. Recent listings put a Basic tier around $7 to $10 per month and a Pro tier, with commercial rights and reference-file uploads, around $15 per month, per AlternativeTo. Third-party retailers have also sold perpetual licenses outside that subscription structure: Thomann lists Basic and Pro perpetual licenses in the $250 to $350 range, and plugin marketplace KVR Audio has discounted the Pro tier well below its list price during promotions. For a producer who expects to use the tool for more than a year, a discounted perpetual license can break even against the monthly subscription in well under twelve months.

What the workflow actually rewards

Vague prompts produce usable but generic results. Specific prompts, with instrumentation, mood, key, and rhythmic detail spelled out, produce results worth keeping. The same logic applies to Accompany: feeding it a dense, fully arranged session confuses the harmonic analysis, while muting everything except the chord track produces a cleaner, more targeted result. Reference material also outperforms description for anything rhythmically unusual, especially genres built on syncopation that a text prompt alone tends to flatten out.

Three things worth asking for next

[ A HARMONIC RISK DIAL ]

The model's biggest recurring critique, mine included, is that it defaults to safe, diatonic chord choices. A dial that intentionally pushes toward modal interchange and less predictable voicings would turn the one soft spot into a real feature for producers chasing something stranger.

[ A LOCAL BUFFER / OFFLINE MODE ]

Every generation requires a live connection. A short local cache of the last several ideas from a session, or a limited offline mode built on top of it, would remove the one real friction point in an otherwise frictionless workflow.

[ STYLE MEMORY FOR ARRANGEMENTS ]

Staccato already claims to adapt to a user's style over time. Take that further: let it remember specific style selections and past sessions, then proactively offer new arrangement directions when a producer is stuck, instead of only reacting to what's typed.

The honest verdict

Staccato earns its place as a workflow tool, not a novelty. It won't write a song for you, and it won't replace the ear you built over years behind a board. What it does is remove the blank timeline problem, the eight-bar loop that never becomes a bridge, the chord progression you can hear in your head but can't quite land on. It is the closest thing to a session player who never gets tired and never rolls their eyes at a weird prompt, and for that, it has earned a permanent slot in the template.
